The problem

Wave data stops where it's needed most

Global wave hindcasts are computed offshore, in deep water. Ports, beaches and coastal designs need the waves that actually reach the coast.

  • Offshore hindcasts give decades of hourly waves, but on a coarse grid, tens of km out at sea.
  • Between there and the coast, the seabed changes the waves: they bend (refraction), steepen (shoaling), get blocked by headlands and islands (sheltering), and break.
  • A spectral wave model resolves all of that, but running it for every hour of 45 years over a whole coastline costs too much to do routinely.
  • So most coastal sites end up using offshore numbers directly, or a single point study that can't be reused anywhere else.

Three ways to get there

Numerical, statistical, or hybrid

Each approach trades accuracy against cost differently. The hybrid one keeps the physics and makes it cheap to use.

Numerical

Run the wave model (SWAN, SnapWave…) for every hour. Accurate, but decades × a whole coastline is too slow for routine use.

Statistical

Fit relations directly between offshore and measured nearshore waves. Fast, but it only works where you already have long measurements, and it can't be moved to another site.

Hybrid — HyWaves

Run the physics on a small, well-chosen sample of sea states. Train a statistical model on those runs, then use it for everything else, anywhere in the domain.

The Sketch

Every goal gets its own training set

Goal points sit on the offshore boundary. Each one only samples directions that can actually reach the coast from it, so no runs are spent on waves travelling out to sea.

The output

Swells and wind sea, transformed into directional spectra

Real sea states mix swells from different storms and local wind sea. HyWaves propagates each component on its own, then adds them up at the coast.

  • The offshore hindcast (WHACS) splits each hour into up to 4 partitions: one wind sea plus three swells.
  • Each partition goes through the metamodel on its own, as a simple sea state from one direction.
  • The nearshore spectrum is the linear sum of the transformed partitions, so two swells arriving from different directions still appear as two peaks at the coast.
  • A cheaper bulk mode (one sea state per hour) is also available.

Before the results

What this first version simplifies, on purpose

Three choices keep HyWaves cheap and general, and each costs some accuracy. Keep them in mind for the numbers that follow: most of the errors you will see come from here, and each one has a known fix. (apparently)

1

Unit wave height (Hs = 1 m)

Every training run uses Hs = 1 m, and the real wave height only rescales the result. That removes one sampling dimension, but 1 m waves rarely break, so depth-limited breaking in storms is missed.

Effect: storm waves overestimated right at the shoreline (5 m contour).
Fix: sample Hs too, or apply a breaking limit.

2

One model per goal, thousands of sites

Each goal's metamodel learns the whole response map at once: about 1,200 sites along three depth contours, compressed with PCA. Detail that matters at one single site can end up in the variance PCA throws away.

Effect: a general model, not tuned to any one location.
Fix: for a project, train on just the couple of sites of interest.

3

One score per depth contour

Validation computes one RMSE over every site and hour on a contour. That number cannot tell a contour that is mediocre everywhere from one that is near-perfect with a few bad spots.

Effect: it hides where the error sits, and how good the good sites are.
Fix: look site by site.

Does it learn the physics?

A few hundred runs are "enough"

  • Each point is the median error (RMSE) across every goal and every depth contour in the case. Switch between wave height and direction.
  • With ~450 training runs, all three regions reproduce SnapWave to within 3–4 cm of Hs and 7–11° of mean direction.
  • Knowing where the curve flattens tells us how much physics each new region needs.

Check 3 · The real test: measured waves

Closer to the buoys than the offshore hindcast

At nearshore buoys, the error of HyWaves (spectral metamodel) is compared with the simplest alternative: using the nearest offshore hindcast point as-is.

The gain is largest where the seabed transforms the waves most: tidal inlets, sheltered bays, long shallow approaches. At exposed buoys in open water the offshore hindcast is already good, and downscaling adds little or nothing.

  • Every case is self-contained: it carries its own copy of the engine, so it keeps running when the main code changes.
  • A case contract defines what makes a folder a HyGloW case, whether it lives in this repo or in yours.

  • Built on BlueMath (statistics) and SnapWave (physics), both open.

Being honest

Where it struggles, and what's next

A fast surrogate inherits the limits of its physics and its forcing. These are the current weak spots.

Known limits

  • Mean direction against buoys: no gain yet over the offshore hindcast on average, even though it closely follows the full simulation.
  • The v1 simplifications: unit Hs (no breaking), one model for thousands of sites, and one score per contour. Their cost shows up at very shallow, sheltered sites (5 m, inside inlets, behind Samoa's reefs).
  • It can only be as good as the offshore hindcast. In open water, downscaling adds little.
  • Summing partitions is linear, so nonlinear interactions between wave systems are ignored.

Next

  • HyWaves v2: sample Hs (or add a breaking limit), site-focused metamodels, and per-site scores in the portal.
  • More regions in HyGloW, contributed by other teams through the same case contract.
  • Beyond hindcasts: forecasts and climate projections through the same trained HyWaves metamodels, with no new physics runs.
  • Nearshore waves as input to impact models: run-up, coastal flooding, erosion.
  • A public deployment of the Results Explorer.
